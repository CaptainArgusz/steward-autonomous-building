# Insights — what a year of PLATO-40 teaches

Level 3 feedback: recommendations for **PLATO** (how to build the next one) and **CLARA** (how to
operate), followed by the target architecture.

> All findings concern the fictional PLATO-40 reference building and its synthetic data. They
> are not statements about real PLATO buildings. Benefits are qualitative or labelled as
> estimates; nothing here is a measured saving.

Generated by `uv run python -m steward.insights data/dev` from the **dev** dataset
(2026-01-01 to 2027-01-01, 365 days). Every number below is computed by
[src/steward/insights.py](src/steward/insights.py); the tables behind them are written to `out/insights/`.
Do not edit the findings by hand - change the analysis and regenerate.

**How to read the statistics.** Rooms are the unit of comparison (40 rooms, 10 per floor, 20 per
orientation). A difference is given with a 95 % bootstrap interval over rooms and a permutation
p-value. This is hindsight analysis of the whole dataset; it is not used for detection.

## For PLATO — design of the next building

### P-01 · Floor 1 loses heat that floors 2-4 do not

**Finding.** In occupied PLATO-40 rooms during heating weather (outdoor < 5 °C, window closed, more than 6 h after check-in) floor 1 sits 0.51 K below its setpoint on average, floors 2-4 only 0.05 K. Difference between room means: +0.45 K (95 % bootstrap CI +0.29 to +0.59, permutation p < 0.001, 10 vs 30 rooms). Floor 1 is below the 20 °C comfort limit in 17% of those readings against 10% elsewhere (+6.4 percentage points (95 % bootstrap CI +1.7 to +11.2, permutation p = 0.011, 10 vs 30 rooms)). The gap widens as it gets colder: +0.63 K at below -5 °C, +0.50 K at -5 to 0 °C, +0.41 K at 0 to 5 °C. 8 of the 10 floor-1 rooms are colder than 90 % of all other rooms (least affected: R101, R104). 150 of 160 ROOM_TEMP_LOW alarms are on floor 1.

**Evidence.** Mean of per-room means, floor 1 (10 rooms) against floors 2-4 (30 rooms), same hours, same plant. Sources: room_telemetry.csv: setpoint_c - temperature_c, 187,010 occupied readings at outdoor < 5 °C; events.jsonl: check-in/check-out for occupancy and time since check-in; weather.csv: outdoor_c bins; events.jsonl: bms_alarm ROOM_TEMP_LOW (150 of 160 on floor 1); out/insights/floor1_heat_loss_by_room.csv.

**Recommendation.** Treat the floor slab over the open parking deck as an external envelope element in the next building: raise its insulation level and check the thermal bridges at the supports, and size the floor-1 heat emitters for the extra loss instead of using the same module on every floor.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: floor 1 reaches the same comfort as the other floors without guests raising setpoints; fewer low-temperature alarms. No energy saving is claimed - heat is not metered per room.

**Uncertainty.** The data proves that floor 1 is colder and that the gap grows with falling outdoor temperature, which is the signature of extra heat loss rather than of a control offset. It does not prove WHERE the heat goes: the open parking deck below is our hypothesis, building.json has no envelope data. Holds within every orientation and room size (orientation=N: +0.59 K; orientation=S: +0.31 K; area_m2=22: +0.36 K; area_m2=28: +0.52 K; area_m2=32: +0.51 K). Plant faults hit all floors at once and cannot produce a floor difference. Guests barely notice so far (6 of 29 'too cold' requests come from floor 1). A slab surface temperature sensor or a thermographic survey would settle the cause. Confidence 0.85.

### P-02 · Rooms overheat in a hot spell and the building has no way to cool them

**Finding.** 'Too hot' is the most frequent comfort complaint in PLATO-40: 201 requests against 29 'too cold' over the whole dataset, all of them in Jun, Jul. In Jun occupied rooms were above 26 °C 22% of the time (mean 24.5 °C) and Jul occupied rooms were above 26 °C 71% of the time (mean 27.2 °C). On 29 days the average occupied room stayed above 26 °C around the clock (worst day 30.6 °C); daily complaints follow the daily indoor mean (r = 0.88). South rooms are warmer than north rooms by +0.56 K (95 % bootstrap CI +0.35 to +0.77, permutation p < 0.001, 20 vs 20 rooms) and complain more (14.3 vs 10.2 requests per 100 occupied nights; +4.1 per 100 nights (95 % bootstrap CI +1.8 to +6.4, permutation p < 0.001, 20 vs 20 rooms)). building.json states cooling: 'none (heating-only heat pump; ventilation provides night flushing in summer)'.

**Evidence.** Occupied readings by month; per-room means, south (20 rooms) against north (20 rooms), in the hot months. Sources: room_telemetry.csv: temperature_c in occupied rooms, months [6, 7]; guest_requests.csv: request_type=temperature_hot (201), temperature_cold (29); building.json: operations.cooling; out/insights/overheating_by_room.csv, overheating_by_month.csv.

**Recommendation.** Plan summer comfort into the next building instead of relying on ventilation alone: external shading on the south façade as standard, and a reversible heat pump (or at least prepared connections for active cooling) so that the existing hydronic system can cool in a heat wave.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: removes the largest single source of comfort complaints in the dataset. Shading addresses the south/north difference; only active cooling addresses the building-wide level.

**Uncertainty.** One summer with one hot spell: the size of the effect depends on the weather that year. The south/north difference is consistent across rooms but small next to the building-wide overheating, so shading alone will not be enough. Floor level makes no measurable difference (top floor vs others +0.03 K, p = 0.87) - the roof is not the cause. The synthetic thermal model is uncalibrated; absolute temperatures are illustrative. Confidence 0.80.

### P-03 · Noise complaints concentrate in the same room positions on the upper floors

**Finding.** 12 of 40 PLATO-40 rooms produce 75% of all 173 noise complaints while accounting for 31% of occupied nights: 4.8 complaints per 100 occupied nights against 0.7 in the other rooms. They are not scattered: all sit at positions 04, 05, 07, 08 on floors 2, 3, 4 (12 of the 12 rooms in that block) - the same positions on every upper floor, on both sides of the corridor. The same positions on floor 1 are quiet. 124 complaints name the neighbouring room, 49 the corridor; 100% arrive between 21:00 and 06:00.

**Evidence.** Complaints per 100 occupied nights per room; hotspot rooms selected from the data (>= 2.5x the median room). Sources: guest_requests.csv: request_type=noise_complaint (173), free text names corridor or neighbour; events.jsonl: stays for occupied nights per room; building.json: room positions (x01-x05 south, x06-x10 north); out/insights/noise_by_room.csv.

**Recommendation.** Review what the modules at these positions on the upper floors have in common in the PLATO layout (stair and lift core, module joints, party-wall build-up, door type) and raise the acoustic specification of party walls and room doors there. Until then CLARA should avoid these rooms for late arrivals and long stays when the house is not full.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: complaints in the affected block would fall towards the level of the other rooms (about 0.7 per 100 nights) if the cause is constructional.

**Uncertainty.** The hotspot rooms were found by looking at the data, so the permutation test (+4.0 per 100 nights (95 % bootstrap CI +3.3 to +4.7, permutation p < 0.001, 12 vs 28 rooms)) overstates the significance; the regular spatial pattern (same positions, three floors, both sides) is the stronger argument. building.json has no floor plan, so what sits at these positions and the cause - core, joints or wall build-up - is a hypothesis. Complaints measure annoyance, not sound level; a sound insulation measurement between two of these rooms would settle it. Confidence 0.70.

### P-04 · The rooftop air handling unit cannot be serviced without a lift platform

**Finding.** 2 of 2 postponed AHU1 work orders in PLATO-40 were postponed for access, in the technician's words: "AHU1 filter change postponed - rooftop unit only reachable with lift platform, not available". The planned filter changes then happened 9 and 5 days after their scheduled date. Over the dataset the filters were changed 2 times as planned and 2 times unplanned ("heavily loaded"); before a planned change the filter pressure drop peaked at 219 Pa, before an unplanned one at 278 Pa (clean filter: about 88 Pa). 19 FILTER_DP_HIGH / AIRFLOW_LOW alarms were raised.

**Evidence.** Work-order log against the daily median of AHU1 filter differential pressure. Sources: work_orders.csv: asset_id=AHU1, status=postponed, summary text; work_orders.csv: AHU1 preventive vs corrective filter replacements; equipment.csv: AHU1 filter_dp_pa, daily median; out/insights/filter_cycles.csv.

**Recommendation.** Give the rooftop plant permanent, safe access in the next building - a roof hatch with fixed ladder or stair and a walkway to the unit - or place the air handling unit in a plant room that is reachable from inside. Filter changes are the most frequent maintenance task on the unit and must not depend on hiring a lift platform.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: planned filter changes happen on the planned day, which removes the access-related delays seen here and the platform hire for every service visit.

**Uncertainty.** Small numbers: 2 postponements in the dataset, read from free-text work orders that people wrote. The access problem itself is stated explicitly, not inferred. The link to the unplanned replacements is NOT proven - they occurred later in the year and may simply reflect faster filter loading in those periods (see C-05). Confidence 0.75.

### P-05 · One floor gateway (GW_F3) drops out repeatedly and takes the whole floor's sensors with it

**Finding.** All ten rooms of floor 3 of PLATO-40 lost every sensor at the same moment 11 times (53 h in total, 5.0 h per outage in the median, spread over 6 different months - about 0.9 per month). The other three floors together had 3 such outages (9 h). 114 of 159 SENSOR_COMM_LOSS alarms concern floor 3. During an outage the building is blind on that floor: no temperature, CO₂, presence or window state for any of its rooms.

**Evidence.** Intervals with all room sensors of a floor missing simultaneously, per floor gateway. Sources: room_telemetry.csv: simultaneous blanks across all rooms of one floor; building.json: sensors -> gateway mapping (one gateway per floor); events.jsonl: bms_alarm SENSOR_COMM_LOSS (159); out/insights/gateway_outages.csv.

**Recommendation.** Do not hang a whole floor on a single gateway: give each floor two gateways with overlapping coverage (or a wired backhaul for the safety-relevant sensors), and buffer readings at the sensor so that short outages are back-filled. On PLATO-40, inspect GW_F3 (power supply, placement, firmware) - a recurring fault on one unit points at the unit or its location.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: removes single points of failure that blinded a floor for about 53 h in this dataset; leak and comfort detection keep working during a fault.

**Uncertainty.** The outages are a direct observation, not an inference. Why this gateway fails is unknown - the data cannot tell a hardware fault from poor radio placement, so we cannot say whether the lesson is 'this unit' or 'this location in the module'. The second most affected floor (3 outages) shows the problem is not strictly unique. Confidence 0.85.

## For CLARA — operating rules

### C-01 · Ventilation is throttled at night exactly when the outside air could cool the rooms

**Finding.** On the 29 days when the average occupied PLATO-40 room was above 26 °C, the outdoor air between 00:00 and 06:00 was 4.9 K cooler than the rooms (22.7 °C outside, 27.5 °C inside). In those same hours AHU1 ran at 3,091 m³/h, below its daytime 4,066 m³/h (nominal 4,200 m³/h). The night setback saves fan energy in winter but in a hot spell it gives away the only cooling the building has. Rooms with an open window were 0.3 K cooler at night than rooms with a closed one.

**Evidence.** Hourly means of AHU1 airflow, outdoor and occupied-room temperature on hot days; night (00-06) against day (08-20). Sources: equipment.csv: AHU1 airflow_m3h by local hour on hot days; weather.csv: outdoor_c; room_telemetry.csv: temperature_c, window_open in occupied rooms; building.json: operations.cooling ('ventilation provides night flushing in summer'); out/insights/night_flushing_by_hour.csv.

**Recommendation.** Replace the fixed night setback with a rule: when the forecast or the measured occupied-room mean exceeds 26 °C and outdoor air is at least 3 K cooler than the rooms, run AHU1 at full airflow with the heat recovery bypassed until 06:00; and message guests in overheated rooms that opening the window at night helps.

**Expected benefit.** Estimate, not a measurement: the withheld airflow of about 975 m³/h at 4.9 K corresponds to roughly 1.6 kW of free sensible cooling during those hours - useful but small against a full house, so this mitigates P-02 and does not replace it.

**Uncertainty.** The schedule is observed directly. The cooling effect is a physics estimate on an uncalibrated synthetic model, and extra fan energy and night noise are not counted. The window comparison is observational - guests who open windows may differ in other ways. The data does not show the state of the heat-recovery bypass. Confidence 0.65.

### C-02 · A robot pass before the cleaner saves cleaner time in every room - and is skipped most on the busiest days

**Finding.** Across 3,714 room cleanings in PLATO-40 the cleaner needed 40 min on average when a robot had cleaned the floor first and 53 min when not (medians 45 vs 60 min). Comparing only like with like - same room size, same cleaner - the saving is 12.3 min per room (raw difference 12.1 min, 95 % CI 11.1 to 13.0, bootstrap over days). Robots went first in 83% of cleanings, but only 71% on the busiest 10 % of days (≥ 15 cleanings) against 87% otherwise.

**Evidence.** Human cleaning duration with vs without a robot floor-clean of the same room in the 8 h before, stratified by room area and cleaner. Sources: events.jsonl: cleaning_started / cleaning_completed (duration, resource_id); events.jsonl: robot_floor_cleaning_completed in the same room within 8 h before; building.json: room area; out/insights/cleaning_minutes_by_stratum.csv.

**Recommendation.** Schedule robots from the checkout list, not from the cleaner's route: as soon as a room is vacated send a robot ahead, most urgently on days with many departures or a sick cleaner, and plan cleaner time with the measured durations (40 min after a robot, 53 min without) instead of one flat figure.

**Expected benefit.** Estimate: at 12.3 min per room the robots already save about 632 cleaner-hours per year; the 621 cleanings without a robot pass are another 127 h, concentrated on the days when cleaner time is scarcest.

**Uncertainty.** Observational: rooms are not randomly assigned to robots. The saving is stable across room sizes, cleaners and floors, which argues against a selection effect, but a dirtier-than-usual room could both miss its robot slot and take longer. Durations are recorded in 15-minute steps, so single cleanings are coarse; only averages are meaningful. Robots cannot enter occupied rooms or clean bathrooms, which caps coverage below 100 %. Confidence 0.80.

### C-03 · Guests wait for rooms on predictable days: many departures per cleaner, or a cleaner absent

**Finding.** 24 arriving guests of PLATO-40 found their room not ready and waited 45 min in the median (longest 750 min). 92% of them arrived before the 15:00 check-in time, and 11 of 24 on a Sat. The waits fall on 16 days that were foreseeable the evening before: a day with a cleaner absent had a waiting guest with probability 29% against 2.6% on other days; the 10 % of days with the most departures per cleaner on duty (≥ 7.0) had one with 18% against 2.5%. Those two conditions together mark 45 days and contain 16 of the 24 waits. There were 349 early check-in requests over the same period.

**Evidence.** Days with at least one arrival_waiting_room event against all other days, by departures per scheduled cleaner and cleaner absence. Sources: events.jsonl: arrival_waiting_room (24) -> checkin_after_wait (wait duration); events.jsonl: checkout / checkout_early per local day; staff_roster.csv: cleaners scheduled, sick or on vacation per day; out/insights/arrival_waits_by_day.csv.

**Recommendation.** Add a next-day turnaround check at roster close: if departures per available cleaner reach 7 or a cleaner is reported absent, CLARA (1) sends robots ahead into every vacated room, (2) orders rooms by arrival time and early check-in requests instead of by floor, (3) answers early check-in requests with a realistic time rather than a default yes, and (4) asks a human to approve extra cleaning capacity.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: the rule would have raised a flag on 45 days a year and covers 16 of the 24 recorded waits. Whether a flagged wait is actually avoided depends on capacity that the replay cannot test.

**Uncertainty.** Only 24 events, so the probabilities are rough. The threshold was read off this year's data and will need re-tuning. Most waiting guests arrived before official check-in, so part of the 'problem' is early arrival rather than late cleaning - the data does not say whether those guests had been promised an early room. Confidence 0.70.

### C-04 · Most BMS alarms repeat one root cause room by room

**Finding.** PLATO-40's building management system raised 464 alarms. ROOM_TEMP_LOW was raised 160 times; 94% of them on floor 1 - the structural heat loss of P-01, not 160 separate incidents - and 98% in rooms that were vacant at that moment. The five most affected rooms account for 76%. ROOM_TEMP_HIGH was raised 125 times on 19 days across 40 rooms - one building-wide heat event reported room by room, for which no action exists (no cooling). SENSOR_COMM_LOSS was raised 159 times, 87% of them as bursts of 8 or more rooms of one floor in the same interval (14 gateway outages reported as 139 room alarms). Grouping room alarms by code, floor and day leaves 187 alarm groups instead of 444 room alarms.

**Evidence.** Each raised bms_alarm joined to occupancy at that moment, floor, and simultaneous alarms on the same floor. Sources: events.jsonl: bms_alarm raised/cleared (464 raised); events.jsonl: occupancy at the time of the alarm; out/insights/alarm_summary.csv.

**Recommendation.** Alarm on causes, not on symptoms: (1) raise one gateway alarm when 8 or more rooms of a floor lose communication together, and suppress the room alarms; (2) evaluate ROOM_TEMP_LOW against the room's own setpoint and occupancy - a vacant room in 18 °C setback is not an alarm - and give floor 1 a persistence time until P-01 is fixed; (3) roll ROOM_TEMP_HIGH up into one building heat event that triggers the night-flush rule of C-01.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: operators see on the order of 187 grouped events instead of 444 room alarms, each pointing at something that can be acted on.

**Uncertainty.** The alarm rules themselves are not in the data; we only see when they fired. BMS alarms in this dataset are stated to be delayed, incomplete and sometimes false, so counts are indicative. Suppression must never hide a single cold occupied room - the rule should group, not mute. Confidence 0.75.

### C-05 · Change the ventilation filters on pressure trend, not on the calendar

**Finding.** AHU1 of PLATO-40 went through 5 filter lives. A clean filter starts at about 88 Pa; lives lasted 40 to 97 days and ended at 201 to 287 Pa. The loading rate ranged from 1.5 to 3.4 Pa per day between lives, so a fixed interval is either too early or too late: 2 changes were unplanned ('heavily loaded') and the BMS raised FILTER_DP_HIGH 10 times. A trigger at twice the clean-filter pressure (176 Pa) would have fired 64 days (median) before the filters were actually changed in 4 of 4 filter lives.

**Evidence.** Filter lives segmented at each drop of the daily median pressure; linear loading rate per life. Sources: equipment.csv: AHU1 filter_dp_pa (daily median), airflow_m3h; work_orders.csv: AHU1 filter replacements, planned and unplanned; out/insights/filter_cycles.csv.

**Recommendation.** Order the filter change when the daily median pressure drop passes about 176 Pa or when the fitted trend will reach it within the technician's lead time, whichever is first; keep the calendar date only as a backstop. Combine the order with access planning (P-04) and with the technician's roster so that the appointment is feasible.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: filter changes become planned work with lead time instead of unplanned call-outs; lightly loaded filters are not replaced early.

**Uncertainty.** 5 filter lives is a small sample and the 2x threshold is a common rule of thumb, not a manufacturer value - the right limit is on the filter's data sheet. Pressure drop also depends on airflow; it was normalised only by using the daily median. Why some lives load faster (pollen, construction dust, season) cannot be seen in the data. Confidence 0.65.

### C-06 · Drinks stock-outs and waste overflows follow the fixed service schedule, not demand

**Finding.** The PLATO-40 vending machine ran out 20 times, every time of drinks (350 hours with zero drinks; snacks never ran out). 14 of the 20 stock-outs happened on a Sun and 95% between May and September: the machine is restocked 'external vendor Mon + Thu 10:00 local; extra restock can be ordered (~24 h lead time)', so the weekend is the longest gap and warm weather the highest demand. The central waste container reached 100 % in 3 pickup cycles (72 hours at the cap); the three days up to each overflow saw 34, 61, 53 departures against a typical 30.

**Evidence.** Stock-out and bin-full events by weekday, season and preceding departures, against the fixed service schedule in building.json. Sources: events.jsonl: vending_out_of_stock (20), waste_bin_full (3); logistics.csv: VEND1 stock_drinks / stock_snacks, WASTE_MAIN fill_pct; building.json: VEND1.restock, WASTE_MAIN.pickup schedules and capacities; events.jsonl: departures per day.

**Recommendation.** Make both services demand-driven: order an extra drinks restock for Friday when the weekend forecast is warm or occupancy is high (24 h lead time), and order an extra waste pickup when booked departures up to the next regular pickup are far above normal. For the next building, a larger drinks compartment than 100 units would remove the dependency on an extra delivery.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: avoids most of the 350 hours without drinks and the 72 hours with a full container seen in this dataset, at the cost of a handful of extra service orders.

**Uncertainty.** 3 overflows is very few - the waste rule is a plausible pattern, not a tested one. Fill levels are capped at 100 %, so the size of an overflow is unknown. Vending demand is modelled, and lost sales during a stock-out are not observable. Confidence 0.70.

### C-07 · In heating weather many guests walk into a room below the comfort band - most often on floor 1

**Finding.** At 1,224 check-ins in heating weather (outdoor < 10 °C) the PLATO-40 room was below the 20 °C comfort limit 24% of the time when the guest arrived: 33% on floor 1 against 20% on floors 2-4 (difference between room means +12.6 percentage points (95 % bootstrap CI +7.6 to +18.1, permutation p < 0.001, 10 vs 30 rooms)). Two hours later 19% of floor-1 rooms and 4% of the others were still below 20 °C. Three hours before arrival 38% of the rooms were still in deep setback (below 19 °C): rooms are heated up for the booked 15:00 check-in, not for the guest.

**Evidence.** Room temperature at the first reading after each actual check-in, floor 1 against floors 2-4, outdoor < 10 °C. Sources: events.jsonl: checkin / checkin_early / checkin_after_wait (actual arrival time); room_telemetry.csv: temperature_c at arrival, 2 h after, 3 h before; building.json: comfort band, setpoint_policy (18 °C setback when unoccupied); out/insights/arrival_temperature_by_floor.csv.

**Recommendation.** Start pre-heating from the room's own warm-up time instead of a fixed clock: begin earlier when it is cold outside and earlier still on floor 1, and trigger it from the cleaning-completed event for rooms with an early check-in request. Pre-heating a vacant room is an autonomous action under the challenge rules.

**Expected benefit.** Qualitative: guests arrive to a room inside the comfort band. Pre-heating costs heat pump electricity; the amount is not estimated here because heat is not metered per room.

**Uncertainty.** The first reading after check-in is up to 15 minutes late, and a guest opening the door or a window can cool the room, so the share is an upper bound. 'Below 20 °C' includes rooms at 19.8 °C; the floor difference is the robust part of the finding. The warm-up dynamics come from an uncalibrated model. Confidence 0.70.

## Checked and not found

Hypotheses we tested that the data does not support.

- **Rooms with a bathtub use much more water.** Bathtub rooms use 168 L per occupied night, the others 162 L: +7 L (95 % bootstrap CI -4 to +18, permutation p = 0.171, 8 vs 32 rooms). A difference of about 4% that is not distinguishable from room-to-room spread.
- **The top floor overheats more than the floors below (roof heat gain).** Top floor against the others in the hot months: +0.03 K (95 % bootstrap CI -0.26 to +0.33, permutation p = 0.867, 10 vs 30 rooms). No roof effect.
- **Room size (22 / 28 / 32 m²) drives comfort problems.** No: hot-month mean temperature 25.9-26.0 °C across the three room sizes; winter shortfall +0.01 to +0.11 K (floors 2-4). Emitters and ventilation scale adequately with room area.
- **North-facing rooms run colder than south-facing rooms in winter.** No (floors 2-4, north minus south shortfall): -0.08 K (95 % bootstrap CI -0.23 to +0.07, permutation p = 0.295, 15 vs 15 rooms).
- **The heat pump is undersized for the coldest days.** Inconclusive, leaning no. Below -5 °C HP1 delivered 55 kW on average and at most 72 kW (nominal 95 kW at +7 °C; there is no capacity measurement at low temperatures). In those hours occupied rooms on floors 2-4 were only +0.08 K off their setpoint, so the plant kept up where the envelope is sound. The comfort problems in cold weather trace to floor 1 (P-01) and to 2 repaired heat pump faults, not to design capacity.
- **PV (60 kWp) and battery (100 kWh) are badly sized for the building.** Not shown. 88% of the 55 MWh PV yield is used on site; export (6.8 MWh) happens only while the battery is full, which is the case 6% of the time. The battery delivers 16.9 MWh a year (about 199 full cycles) and sits empty 27% of the time, mainly in winter when there is no surplus to store. Grid import is 125 MWh: the constraint is winter heating demand, which no realistic battery shifts. No resizing recommendation.
- **Wi-fi complaints cluster on one floor (e.g. the floor with the failing sensor gateway).** No. 102 wi-fi fault requests, 0.90 to 1.47 per 100 occupied nights by floor; the highest floor (2) against the rest: +0.40 per 100 nights (95 % bootstrap CI -0.20 to +1.03, permutation p = 0.163, 10 vs 30 rooms). The sensor gateways and guest wi-fi appear to be independent.
